The EuroGuiDerm guideline is the primary source for every clinical recommendation. Use the others
for practical patient information (timelines, side effects in daily life, scars).

## Definition and pathogenesis (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`)

- Acne vulgaris is "a polymorphic chronic inflammatory skin disease", mainly on the face (99%),
  with "open and/or closed comedones and inflammatory lesions including papules, pustules and
  nodules".
- Four main factors: increased sebum production; altered follicular keratinisation; dysbiosis of
  the skin microbiome (_Cutibacterium acnes_, shift toward the IA1 phylotype and lower diversity);
  release of inflammatory mediators.

## Who gets it (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`, `dermnet-acne`)

- Prevalence in adolescents in Western industrialised countries: "between 50% and 95%"; excluding
  mild forms, "20% - 35%".
- "Acne may persist beyond teen age in a significant proportion of individuals."
- DermNet: acne "affects both sexes and all races"; mainly adolescents, but also adults.
- Psychological impact: "Patients are prone to embarrassment and social withdrawal, depression,
  anxiety and anger." Scars "can produce significant disfigurement and psychosocial impairment in
  their own right."

## Types and severity (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`, `dermnet-acne`)

EuroGuiDerm uses four clinical categories (use these for /acnee/tipuri):

1. Comedonal acne: non-inflamed lesions (open and closed comedones).
2. Mild to moderate papulopustular acne.
3. Severe papulopustular / moderate nodular acne.
4. Severe nodular / conglobate acne: deep nodules over 5 mm, tissue destruction, scarring.

Lesions (DermNet): open comedones (blackheads) and closed comedones (whiteheads), papules,
pustules, nodules, pseudocysts; secondary lesions are scars, red marks and pigmented marks.

Truncal involvement: "More widespread involvement will certainly favour earlier use of systemic
treatment."

Agent notes from the guideline:

- Adapalene "should be selected in preference to tretinoin and isotretinoin" (topical), because of
  tolerability.
- Benzoyl peroxide: two large studies on a suspected benzene/leukaemia link "support the safety of
  the standard use of benzoyl peroxide".
- Azelaic acid: better tolerability than BPO and tretinoin.
- Clindamycin + tretinoin was downgraded to medium "due to general concerns with development of
  antibiotic resistance".
- Topical antibiotic monotherapy "is generally not recommended".
- Clascoterone received EU marketing authorisation on 21 October 2025 but is not yet in the
  guideline algorithm. Do not write about it.
- Pregnancy: options that "can be considered" are topical azelaic acid and BPO (± clindamycin or
  erythromycin); systemic zinc or azithromycin.

## Antibiotics: stewardship (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`)

- Preferred oral antibiotics: doxycycline and lymecycline, "in preference to minocycline and
  tetracycline". Minocycline has more severe adverse events (hypersensitivity, liver problems,
  lupus-like syndrome).
- Doxycycline causes photosensitivity, dependent on dose and sun exposure; lymecycline has lower
  phototoxicity.
- "Limit the use of systemic antibiotics for the treatment of acne to a period of three months."
  Longer only as an exception, when topicals are insufficient and isotretinoin or hormonal
  treatment is not suitable.
- In the guideline, systemic antibiotics appear only in combination with a topical (adapalene,
  azelaic acid, adapalene + BPO). Write it that way: never an antibiotic on its own.

## Isotretinoin (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`, `ema-retinoids-2018`, `nhs-acne-treatment`)

- Strongly recommended for severe papulopustular / moderate nodular and severe nodular /
  conglobate acne. EuroGuiDerm: "greatest effectiveness" of all options.
- EMA 2018: oral retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin, alitretinoin) "must be used in accordance
  with the conditions of a new pregnancy prevention programme by women able to have children":
  at least one effective method of contraception during and after treatment, pregnancy tests
  before, during and after treatment, an acknowledgement form and a patient reminder card.
- Neuropsychiatric: data could not establish whether the risk is due to retinoids, but a warning
  about possible depression, anxiety and mood changes was added to oral retinoid labels as a
  precaution.
- Topical retinoids: contraindicated in pregnancy; no neuropsychiatric warning needed.
- NHS: isotretinoin "can only be prescribed by a specialist doctor".
- Site rule: education only. Telegen does not prescribe isotretinoin.

## What to expect and side effects (`nhs-acne-treatment`)

- "Many of these treatments can take 2 to 3 months before they start to work."
- Benzoyl peroxide: dry, tight skin; burning, itching or stinging; it can bleach hair and
  clothing; it increases sensitivity to sunlight.
- Topical retinoids are "not suitable for use during pregnancy".
- Topical antibiotics: a 6 to 8 week course is usual, then stop to limit resistance.
- Combined contraceptive pill: may take up to a year for the full benefit.
- Do NOT use the NHS oral antibiotic duration (4–6 months): EuroGuiDerm limits it to 3 months.
  See conflicts-and-decisions.md.

## Diet and hygiene (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`)

- "At present there is not enough evidence to support specific diets for treating acne beyond
  recommending a 'balanced diet'."
- High glycaemic load diets and dairy "are associated with" worse acne; association, not proven
  cause. Write it that way.

## Scars and marks (`euroguiderm-acne-2025`, `dermnet-acne-scars`)

- Scarring is "identified in up to 95% of patients attending a dermatology clinic".
- Atrophic scars (collagen loss) are more frequent than hypertrophic or keloid scars. DermNet:
  ice-pick scars are the most common atrophic type (60–70%); boxcar and rolling scars are also
  common.
- "Duration of inflammation relates to scar production"; delay in treatment makes significant
  scarring more likely. "Presence of scarring should support aggressive management and therapy
  should be commenced early."
- DermNet: "Timely appropriate treatment for acne during the active phase can reduce the incidence
  and severity of acne scarring." Scarring is more common in moderate to severe acne; smoking and
  picking may increase the risk.
- Procedures exist (laser and other resurfacing, chemical peels, microneedling, fillers,
  subcision, excision), but "active acne should be treated prior to commencing scar management".
  These are in-person dermatology procedures; Telegen does not offer them.
- "Acne scars are usually permanent, although may improve spontaneously over time or with
  treatment."
- Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation that persists after acne settles "is of great concern to many
  patients".

## When to see a doctor in person

Use for the "Când ai nevoie de consult în persoană" block. Supported by the sources above: nodules
or deep painful lumps (severe categories call for isotretinoin, a specialist medicine), scarring
(treat early), widespread truncal acne (favours systemic treatment), pregnancy or planned pregnancy
(retinoids), low mood (psychological impact; isotretinoin warning). From the owner brief (CLAUDE.md
§7c.C), not from these sources: acne with fever or feeling unwell, and sudden severe acne in
adulthood. Keep them, but the reviewing dermatologist must confirm the final list.
